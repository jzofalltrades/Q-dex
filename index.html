01

What Q-dexter Is

Q-dexter is a real, handheld Pokédex — not a simulation, not a phone app. Point it at a Pokémon card or figure, press SCAN, and an on-device neural network identifies it, registers it to your trainer profile, awards XP, and unlocks badges. Press OAK and ask a question out loud; Professor Oak answers back in his own voice.

It runs a full 1025-Pokémon database, multi-trainer profiles, and an XP & badge system spanning 9 regions — all inside a 3D-printed Gen-1 Kanto enclosure that opens and closes on a real hinge.

🖥️

Display Layer

Waveshare 2.8" HDMI IPS driven directly off the UNO Q's HDMI-out. Zero driver work.

🕹️

Input Layer

ESP32-C3 satellite MCU polls 10 buttons and streams events over USB serial.

🧠

Intelligence Layer

MobileNetV2 vision + whisper.cpp speech, both running locally on the UNO Q.

🖨️

Physical Layer

Fusion 360 enclosure: hinged lid, flush button caps, dome lens, press-fit inserts.

🎯
Who this guide is for

Anyone with a 3D printer and basic soldering skills. Every command is copy-pasteable. Every phase ends with a verification checkpoint so you know it works before moving on — no debugging four problems at once.

02

Why We Built It This Way

Three design decisions define this project. Each solves a real constraint rather than following the obvious path.

Decision 1 — Split the workload: one processor, one controller

The Arduino UNO Q is a Linux board. Linux is not real-time: polling GPIO from Python means missed button presses whenever the scheduler is busy running inference. So the UNO Q does zero GPIO. An ESP32-C3 Super Mini polls all 10 buttons at a steady 50 Hz and streams single-byte events over USB serial. The UNO Q reads those bytes in a daemon thread and drops them into a queue.

Result: button response stays crisp even while the neural network is running. This is the single most important architectural choice in the build.

Decision 2 — Run the AI on-device, not in the cloud

Both the vision model (MobileNetV2 via ai_edge_litert) and the speech model (whisper.cpp, tiny.en) run locally on the UNO Q. No round trip, no API latency, no connectivity requirement for the core scan loop. A real Pokédex works in a cave.

Decision 3 — Make the enclosure a functional part, not a shell

The lid geometry is dimensioned against the button caps: caps sit at z=−3.7 mm, exactly 0.3 mm proud of the recessed panel field at z=−4.0 mm. When the lid rotates 180° closed, the LidFacePlate clears every cap by that 0.3 mm. Six mounting bosses were shortened specifically to make this clearance work. The enclosure closes like the real thing because the CAD was solved as an interference problem, not styled as a box.

💡
Real-world takeaway

This split-workload pattern generalises: any Linux SBC project needing responsive physical input benefits from offloading GPIO to a cheap MCU over serial. The ESP32-C3 Super Mini costs under ₹250 and eliminates an entire class of latency bugs.

Circuit Schematics

There are only two circuits in this build: the keypad matrix-free direct-GPIO wiring on the ESP32-C3, and the USB/HDMI interconnect on the UNO Q. Both are given here at pin level.

5.1 — ESP32-C3 keypad schematic

Each button connects its GPIO pin to GND. The firmware enables the ESP32's internal pull-up, so an unpressed button reads HIGH and a pressed button reads LOW (active-low). A 100 nF capacitor across each button suppresses contact bounce in hardware; the firmware adds a software debounce window on top.

Phase 0 — First Boot & Setup

The Arduino UNO Q ships with Ubuntu already installed — it is a full Linux single-board computer, not a bare microcontroller. So there is no OS to flash; you just need to boot it, connect it to your network, and get terminal access. Do this once, at the start, and every later phase talks to the board over SSH.

Step 1 — First boot like any SBC

For the very first setup, treat the UNO Q like a desktop: connect a monitor (HDMI), a USB keyboard and mouse, and power (5 V 3 A USB-C). It boots straight into the Ubuntu desktop.

💡
You only need the monitor/keyboard once

After the network is set up, everything is done remotely over SSH from your laptop — the board can run "headless" (no monitor, keyboard or mouse attached). The screen and keypad you wire up in later phases are the Pokédex's display and buttons, not for driving Linux.

Step 2 — Connect to Wi-Fi

Use the Ubuntu desktop's Wi-Fi menu (top-right) to join your network, or from a terminal:

BASH — ON THE UNO Q
# List nearby networks
nmcli device wifi list

# Join yours (use your network name and password)
nmcli device wifi connect "YOUR_WIFI_NAME" password "YOUR_WIFI_PASSWORD"
📶
Prefer the 2.4 GHz band

A handheld device moves around, and 2.4 GHz reaches further than 5 GHz. If your router shows both, join the 2.4 GHz one for a more stable connection.

Step 3 — Find the board's IP address

BASH — ON THE UNO Q
hostname -I

The first address it prints (for example 192.168.1.56) is the board's address on your network. Write it down — you use it to connect from your laptop.

Step 4 — Connect from your laptop over SSH

From here on you can unplug the monitor, keyboard and mouse and work entirely from your laptop. On Windows, MobaXterm is a free, friendly SSH client (macOS and Linux can use the built-in ssh command). Start a new SSH session to the board's IP, logging in as the arduino user:

BASH — ON YOUR LAPTOP
ssh arduino@192.168.1.56    # use YOUR board's IP

The default user is arduino. You will be asked for its password on first connect. MobaXterm also gives you drag-and-drop file transfer and X11 forwarding, which is handy when the physical display is not connected yet.

▶ CHECKPOINT 0
  • UNO Q boots to the Ubuntu desktop
  • Board is connected to Wi-Fi (preferably 2.4 GHz)
  • hostname -I shows the board's IP
  • You can SSH in from your laptop and land at the arduino@ prompt

Phase 2 — Keypad & Serial Link

Now build the input layer. The ESP32-C3 is flashed once and then never touched again — all application logic lives on the UNO Q.

7.1 — Firmware

Flash with Arduino IDE (board: ESP32C3 Dev Module) or PlatformIO. The firmware does three jobs: poll, debounce, encode.

C++ — qdexter_keypad.ino
/*
 * Q-dexter Keypad Controller — ESP32-C3 Super Mini
 * Polls 10 buttons at 50 Hz, sends 1 ASCII byte per press over USB Serial.
 *
 * Wiring: each button connects its GPIO to GND.
 *         Internal pull-ups are enabled, so buttons are ACTIVE LOW.
 * Note:   GPIO 8 and 9 are strapping pins and are deliberately unused.
 */

#define NUM_BTN     10
#define DEBOUNCE_MS 40
#define LED_PIN      2      // safe as an output

// GPIO for each button, in the same order as CODES[]
const uint8_t PINS[NUM_BTN] = {
   0,   1,   3,   4,   5,   6,   7,  10,  20,  21
};

// Byte sent to the UNO Q when the matching button is pressed
const char CODES[NUM_BTN] = {
  'U', 'D', 'L', 'R', 'A', 'B', 'S', 's', 'C', 'O'
};

bool     lastStable[NUM_BTN];    // last accepted level
uint32_t lastChange[NUM_BTN];    // millis of last accepted change

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);

  for (uint8_t i = 0; i < NUM_BTN; i++) {
    pinMode(PINS[i], INPUT_PULLUP);
    lastStable[i] = HIGH;        // released
    lastChange[i] = 0;
  }

  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, HIGH);   // solid = keypad ready
}

void loop() {
  uint32_t now = millis();

  for (uint8_t i = 0; i < NUM_BTN; i++) {
    bool level = digitalRead(PINS[i]);

    // ignore any change inside the debounce window
    if (now - lastChange[i] < DEBOUNCE_MS) continue;

    if (level != lastStable[i]) {
      lastStable[i] = level;
      lastChange[i] = now;

      // falling edge (HIGH -> LOW) == press
      if (level == LOW) {
        Serial.write(CODES[i]);
        Serial.flush();
      }
    }
  }

  delay(20);   // 50 Hz poll
}

Why active-low

The ESP32-C3's internal pull-ups are far more reliable than its pull-downs, and wiring every switch common to a single GND rail means one shared wire instead of ten routed to 3V3. Inside a hinged enclosure with limited wire space, that difference matters.

7.2 — Host-side serial reader

On the UNO Q, a daemon thread owns the serial port and pushes decoded events into a queue. The pygame loop drains that queue each frame. The read never blocks rendering.

PYTHON — serial_keypad.py
"""Keypad bridge: ESP32-C3 USB serial -> thread-safe event queue."""

import queue
import threading
import serial

BAUD = 115200

CODE_MAP = {
    b'U': 'UP',    b'D': 'DOWN',  b'L': 'LEFT',   b'R': 'RIGHT',
    b'A': 'A',     b'B': 'B',     b'S': 'START',  b's': 'SELECT',
    b'C': 'SCAN',  b'O': 'OAK',
}


class Keypad:
    """Owns the serial port; exposes a non-blocking poll()."""

    def __init__(self, port='/dev/ttyACM0', baud=BAUD):
        self.port = port
        self.baud = baud
        self.events = queue.Queue()
        self._stop = threading.Event()
        self._thread = None

    def start(self):
        self._thread = threading.Thread(target=self._run, daemon=True)
        self._thread.start()
        return self

    def _run(self):
        while not self._stop.is_set():
            try:
                with serial.Serial(self.port, self.baud, timeout=0.1) as ser:
                    ser.reset_input_buffer()
                    while not self._stop.is_set():
                        byte = ser.read(1)
                        if byte in CODE_MAP:
                            self.events.put(CODE_MAP[byte])
            except serial.SerialException:
                # cable pulled or device re-enumerated: retry, don't crash
                self._stop.wait(1.0)

    def poll(self):
        """Return the next event, or None. Never blocks."""
        try:
            return self.events.get_nowait()
        except queue.Empty:
            return None

    def stop(self):
        self._stop.set()

The reconnect loop matters: if the ESP32-C3 re-enumerates (common after a USB power blip), a naive reader raises and takes the whole app down. This one waits a second and reopens.

Consuming events in the main loop

PYTHON — usage in main.py
from serial_keypad import Keypad

keypad = Keypad('/dev/ttyACM0').start()

while running:
    # 1. physical keypad
    while (ev := keypad.poll()) is not None:
        current_screen.handle(ev)

    # 2. keyboard fallback for development over SSH
    for e in pygame.event.get():
        if e.type == pygame.KEYDOWN:
            current_screen.handle(KEYBOARD_MAP.get(e.key))

    current_screen.draw(surface)
    pygame.display.flip()
    clock.tick(60)

7.3 — Wiring the buttons

  1. 1
    Build the GND rail first

    Cut one length of black 26 AWG wire. Strip a 2 mm window every 15 mm and solder each button's common leg to those windows. One wire, ten taps — far tidier than ten separate returns.

  2. 2
    Run signal wires

    One wire from each button's other leg to its assigned GPIO per the pin table. Keep every run under 15 cm.

  3. 3
    Add debounce caps

    Solder a 100 nF ceramic directly across each button's two legs. This is optional if the firmware debounce is sufficient, but it noticeably improves feel on cheap switches.

  4. 4
    Solder the status LED

    GPIO 2 → 330 Ω → LED anode; LED cathode → GND rail. Mind the polarity: the longer leg is the anode.

Test the link before assembly

BASH — raw serial monitor
# Confirm the ESP32 enumerated

"

8.3 — Audio configuration

The USB sound card must be addressed as plughw:1,0, never raw hw:1,0. The plug layer performs sample-rate conversion; addressing the raw device returns 44 100 Hz, and whisper.cpp requires 16 000 Hz. This single detail causes more silent failures than anything else in the build.

BASH — verify audio
# Which card index is the USB sound card?
arecord -l

# Record 5 s at 16 kHz mono — the format whisper.cpp needs
arecord -D plughw:1,0 -d 5 -f S16_LE -r 16000 -c 1 /tmp/test.wav

# File should be ~160 KB. If it is ~440 KB, the rate is wrong.
ls -lh /tmp/test.wav

# Transcribe
~/whisper.cpp/build/bin/whisper-cli \
  -m ~/whisper.cpp/models/ggml-tiny.en.bin \
  -f /tmp/test.wav

# Test speech output
espeak "Professor Oak online" 2>/dev/null

8.4 — Code structure

The application is organised so each screen is a self-contained handler. Adding a new screen means adding one file and one route entry — no changes to the event loop.

The Screen contract

Every screen implements the same two methods. This is what keeps the event loop trivial.

PYTHON — screens/base.py
from abc import ABC, abstractmethod


class Screen(ABC):
    """Every screen implements exactly this interface."""

    @abstractmethod
    def handle(self, event: str | None) -> str | None:
        """Process one input event ('A', 'UP', 'SCAN', ...).

        Return the name of the screen to switch to, or None to stay.
        """

    @abstractmethod
    def draw(self, surface) -> None:
        """Render this screen onto the given pygame surface."""

    def on_enter(self) -> None:
        """Optional hook: called once when this screen becomes active."""

    def on_exit(self) -> None:
        """Optional hook: called once when leaving this screen."""

Vision inference

The interpreter is loaded once at startup, not per scan — loading a TFLite interpreter takes hundreds of milliseconds and would make every scan feel sluggish.

PYTHON — core/vision.py
"""On-device Pokemon recognition using MobileNetV2 via LiteRT."""

import numpy as np
from PIL import Image
from ai_edge_litert.interpreter import Interpreter

INPUT_SIZE = (224, 224)


class PokemonVision:
    """Loads the model once and reuses the interpreter for every scan."""

    def __init__(self, model_path, labels_path):
        self.interp = Interpreter(model_path=model_path)
        self.interp.allocate_tensors()
        self.inp = self.interp.get_input_details()[0]
        self.out = self.interp.get_output_details()[0]

        with open(labels_path) as fh:
            self.labels = [line.strip() for line in fh if line.strip()]

    def predict(self, frame_rgb, top_k=3):
        """Return [(label, confidence), ...] sorted best-first."""
        img = Image.fromarray(frame_rgb).resize(INPUT_SIZE)
        arr = np.asarray(img, dtype=np.float32)[None] / 255.0

        self.interp.set_tensor(self.inp['index'], arr)
        self.interp.invoke()
        scores = self.interp.get_tensor(self.out['index'])[0]

        idx = np.argsort(scores)[-top_k:][::-1]
        return [(self.labels[i], float(scores[i])) for i in idx]

Speech pipeline

PYTHON — core/speech.py
"""Offline speech-to-text: arecord -> whisper.cpp -> plain text."""

import re
import subprocess
from pathlib import Path

WHISPER = Path.home() / "whisper.cpp/build/bin/whisper-cli"
MODEL   = Path.home() / "whisper.cpp/models/ggml-tiny.en.bin"
WAV     = Path("/tmp/oak_input.wav")

# whisper-cli prints lines like: [00:00:00.000 --> 00:00:03.100]   text here
LINE_RE = re.compile(r"\[[\d:.]+\s*-->\s*[\d:.]+\]\s*(.*)")


def record(seconds=5):
    """Capture mono 16 kHz audio. plughw is required, not hw."""
    subprocess.run(
        ["arecord", "-D", "plughw:1,0", "-d", str(seconds),
         "-f", "S16_LE", "-r", "16000", "-c", "1", str(WAV)],
        check=True, capture_output=True,
    )
    return WAV


def transcribe(wav_path=WAV):
    """Run whisper.cpp and return the joined transcript."""
    result = subprocess.run(
        [str(WHISPER), "-m", str(MODEL), "-f", str(wav_path), "-nt"],
        check=True, capture_output=True, text=True,
    )

    lines = []
    for raw in result.stdout.splitlines():
        match = LINE_RE.match(raw.strip())
        text = match.group(1) if match else raw.strip()
        if text:
            lines.append(text)

    return " ".join(lines).strip()


def listen(seconds=5):
    record(seconds)
    return transcribe()
🐛
A bug worth knowing about

An earlier version of this module returned an empty string for every utterance. The cause was a missing import re swallowed by a broad except Exception around the call site — the parse failed silently and returned "". Catch specific exceptions, and never let a parse helper return an empty string on failure where a raise would be clearer.

The Physical AI — On-Device Intelligence

Q-dexter is not a thin client that phones a cloud API for every decision. Two AI workloads run directly on the Arduino UNO Q: a computer-vision classifier that identifies Pokémon from the camera, and a speech pipeline that lets Professor Oak listen and talk back. Why MobileNetV2?

It is built for resource-constrained edge hardware — a small CNN using depthwise-separable convolutions that keeps compute low enough to run in real time on the board while still giving strong accuracy from transfer learning.

How the model was trained

StageWhat we did
DatasetCurated an image set for the four Kanto mascots — Bulbasaur, Charizard, Pikachu and Squirtle — covering cards, figures and artwork, split into train / validation.
Pre-processResized to the MobileNetV2 input size, normalised pixel values, and augmented (flips, rotation, zoom, brightness) so the model generalises across lighting and camera angles on the real device.
Transfer learningStarted from ImageNet-pretrained MobileNetV2, froze the convolutional base, and trained a new classification head for the 4 classes — no massive dataset needed to reach usable accuracy.
ExportConverted the trained Keras model to TensorFlow Lite (pokedex_phyai.tflite) so it is small and fast enough for on-device inference.
DeployShipped the .tflite model + labels.txt to the board; the pygame app loads it via ai_edge_litert and runs inference locally on each scan.

Engineering note

USB audio is captured through plughw (not raw hw) so the stream is resampled to the 16 kHz mono format whisper.cpp requires — the difference between garbage transcriptions and clean ones.

9.3 · On-Board Text-to-Speech & the Oak Assistant

The transcribed question is answered by the Professor Oak assistant, and the reply is spoken aloud on the board using the eSpeak engine through the USB speaker. Oak is trainer-aware — he knows which profile is active and can reference the trainer's own XP, badges and progress — and a physical mute button (GPIO 10) cuts the audio instantly mid-sentence for a natural, interruptible conversation.

Why this counts as Physical AI

The only optional network hop is the language reasoning behind Oak's replies; every sensing and acting step lives on the device.

Why negative horizontal expansion

Extruded filament spreads slightly beyond its commanded path, so a modelled 6.0 mm button hole prints at roughly 5.8 mm and the cap will not seat. A −0.10 mm horizontal expansion compensates. Print one button cap and its matching hole as a test coupon before committing to the nine-hour shell print.

9.3 — Assembly sequence

Order matters — several steps become impossible if done out of sequence.

  1. 1
    Dry-fit everything first

    Before any glue or solder, test-fit every printed part together. Sand button holes with 220-grit until caps slide freely. Fixing fit problems after wiring is painful.

  2. 2
    Seat the display in its bezel

    Press the panel in from the front. Run a thin hot-glue bead around the inside edge only — keep glue off the ribbon connector. Let it set fully before handling.

  3. 3
    Mount the UNO Q

    Seat the board on the UNO Q Mount body, rotated 90°. Secure with four M2.5×6 mm screws. Attach the mount into the left shell before any cables are connected.

  4. 4
    Route cables before closing anything

    HDMI first (it is the stiffest and least forgiving), then camera USB, then sound card USB, then the ESP32 link. Leave a 15 mm service loop at the hinge so the lid can open without tension.

  5. 5
    Install buttons and the ESP32-C3

    Press tactile switches through their holes from outside. Hot-glue the ESP32-C3 to a flat interior area of the right shell with its USB-C port facing the cable exit. Solder per §7.3, then press each cap on — light thumb pressure only.

  6. 6
    Mount camera, mic and speaker

    Press-fit the camera into the front lens cutout. Mount the mic near the OAK button side so the user naturally speaks toward it. Position the speaker facing an outlet slot, not into solid wall.

  7. 7
    Fit the shoulder LED and dome lens

    Push the LED through from inside, then snap the clear Dome_Lens over it from outside. It should click without glue. If it is loose, a single drop of CA on the flange is enough.

  8. 8
    Press in the 18 dummy inserts

    Work top row downward. The retention flanges hold them — no adhesive. Going bottom-up tends to misalign the last row.

  9. 9
    Install the hinge pin and test closure

    Push the M3×20 mm pin through all knuckles from the outside. Open and close ten times. Confirm the LidFacePlate never contacts a cap — hold it up to a light and look for the 0.3 mm gap.

  10. 10
    Close up and power on

    Fit six M3×8 mm screws at the shell bosses. Snap the BackPlate onto its perimeter clips last — it is designed to come off again for servicing, so do not glue it.

Extending the project

  • Expand the vision model — retrain MobileNetV2 on more species. The label file and inference wrapper need no code changes, only a new .tflite and labels.txt.
